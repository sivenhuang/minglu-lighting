All-in-One vs Split-Type Solar Street Light: Which Design Fits Your Project in 2026

Expert insights from Minglu Lighting, your solar and LED lighting partner

2026-10-13·9 min·Comparison

When procurement teams shortlist solar street lights, the first fork in the road is mechanical, not electrical: do you specify an all-in-one (integrated) unit, or a split-type (separated) system? Both deliver the same outcome, free off-grid illumination at night, yet they differ sharply in cost structure, thermal behavior, serviceability, and the environments where each one thrives. This guide compares the two architectures on the metrics that actually move a B2B decision, then maps each to its best-fit project profile.

1. What Is an All-in-One Solar Street Light

An all-in-one fixture packs every component into a single factory-sealed housing mounted on top of the pole: the LED module, the lithium battery, the MPPT charge controller, and a monocrystalline panel are bonded together behind a shared enclosure. The name integrated solar street light describes the same product class.

  • Single SKU. One part number covers panel, battery, controller, and lamp. Procurement, shipping, and warranty all collapse to one line.
  • Plug-and-play install. Bolt the unit to the pole, switch it on, done. No separate battery box, no wire harness between pole top and ground.
  • Lower bill of materials. Shared housing and combined assembly labor typically make the all-in-one the cheaper option per delivered lumen.

The two designs also carry different implications for maintenance planning and total cost of ownership. An all-in-one failure typically means replacing the whole luminaire, while a split-type failure usually isolates to one module. Understanding that difference before procurement avoids painful surprises three or four years into a deployment.

The trade-off is structural. Because the panel, battery, and LED share one sealed shell, the battery sits in the same thermal envelope as the panel and the lamp. That coupling drives most of the real-world limitations discussed below.

2. What Is a Split-Type Solar Street Light

A split-type system separates the energy components from the luminaire. The LED lamp head sits at the pole top; the solar panel is mounted on a bracket above or beside it; and the battery lives in its own weatherproof box, usually at the pole base or mid-pole. The three are joined by cabling and external connectors.

  • Independent optimization. You can size the panel and battery to the site without being constrained by the luminaire's physical footprint.
  • Thermal isolation. The battery stays away from the lamp and panel heat, which protects LiFePO4 cells in hot climates.
  • Field serviceability. A failed battery is a box swap at ground level, not a full luminaire replacement at height.

The cost is complexity: more parts, more wiring, more installation labor, and three separate warranty lines to manage. For a single remote light that is overhead. For a 500-unit municipal rollout it is a real line-item.

4. Cost Comparison: Equipment and Lifetime

On a like-for-like 60W-equivalent project, all-in-one wins the upfront line. The gap narrows over the lifetime once you price battery service.

The headline is that the two converge at the 5-year mark. All-in-one saves on hardware and labor up front; split-type saves on the year-5 battery event because you replace a box, not a luminaire. The cheaper choice therefore depends on how you value deployment speed versus service cost.

5. Performance and Reliability Factors

Four physical realities decide which architecture performs better on a given site.

  • Battery temperature. LiFePO4 cells lose capacity and cycle life as ambient temperature rises. In desert or tropical installs, a split-type battery box (often insulated and pole-mounted low) stays cooler than a battery sealed behind a sun-baked all-in-one panel. Cooler cells mean more winter autonomy and a longer service life.
  • Panel orientation. All-in-one panels are locked to the luminaire's plane, so they rarely face the optimal tilt or azimuth. Split-type panels can be aimed at the equator and tilted to the site latitude, recovering 10–25% more daily yield in many regions.
  • Wind and snow load. The compact all-in-one presents a smaller sail area at the pole top, an advantage in hurricane or coastal zones. Split systems add an offset panel that increases moment load; the pole and bracket must be spec'd accordingly.
  • Light uniformity. Because split-type lamps are not constrained by the integrated shell, they more easily accept larger optics and higher outputs for wide roads and highways. All-in-one designs top out around 120W of useful lamp power before the housing becomes unwieldy.

Smart controls and monitoring. Both architectures support time-based dimming and motion sensing, but split-type systems more often expose the controller for remote telemetry, letting operators read state-of-charge and fault codes from a dashboard. All-in-one units are increasingly adding the same connectivity, though the sealed housing can make firmware servicing harder. If central fleet management matters to your operation, confirm the control platform before you commit to either form factor.

6. Installation and Deployment Speed

Speed is where all-in-one is hardest to beat. A two-person crew can stand up an all-in-one in well under an hour per pole: set the foundation, raise the pole, clamp the unit, test. Split-type adds panel bracket alignment, battery box mounting, and connector termination, pushing the same job to two or three times the labor.

For emergency deployments, disaster recovery, or a contractor racing a seasonal deadline, that labor delta is often the deciding factor. For a planned municipal program with a multi-month schedule, the extra install hours matter far less than the lifetime service economics.

8. Sizing and Specifying in a Tender

Whichever architecture you choose, the procurement document should pin down the same fundamentals. Start from the load: lamp wattage, required lumens, and target run-time after the worst-case (winter) day. Then size the battery for autonomy days and the panel for peak-sun recovery. Our battery and panel sizing guide walks through the formulas with worked examples.

Specification checklist:

  • Battery chemistry and cycles. Insist on LiFePO4 rated for 2,000+ cycles, not generic lithium-ion.
  • Charge controller. MPPT, not PWM, for 15–30% more harvest.
  • IP and IK ratings. IP65 minimum for the electronics, IK08+ for vandal-prone public spaces.
  • Photocell and dimming. Motion or time-based dimming extends autonomy and cuts wear.
  • Warranty terms. Separate the panel (20–25 years) from the battery (3–5 years) and the lamp (5 years).

9. Common Mistakes Buyers Make

  • Buying on price per unit alone. The cheap all-in-one can become the expensive option once a sealed battery fails and the whole luminaire must be craned down.
  • Ignoring the battery service path. If you cannot reach the battery without removing the fixture, multiply your year-5 cost by your pole count and your crane rate.
  • Over-tilting the all-in-one expectation. Treat its fixed panel as a constraint; do not assume grid-equivalent yield from a flat-mounted surface.
  • Mismatching pole and wind load. Split systems need a stronger pole and bracket; underspecifying invites failures in the first storm.

Conclusion

Neither architecture is universally superior. The all-in-one (integrated) solar street light wins on first cost, install speed, and compact wind profile, which makes it the default for pathways, parks, campuses, and rapid deployments. The split-type system wins on thermal management, scalable output, adjustable sun capture, and cheap field service, which makes it the stronger choice for hot climates, high-wattage roads, shaded sites, and large municipal fleets.

The disciplined approach is to score each project against the scenario matrix above, then specify the architecture that fits rather than the one your last supplier happened to stock.
