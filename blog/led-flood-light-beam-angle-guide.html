LED Flood Light Beam Angle Guide: Choosing the Right Spread and Throw Distance for 2026

Expert insights from Minglu Lighting — your solar and LED lighting partner

2026-11-03·9 min·Technical Guide

Specifying an LED flood light by wattage alone is a mistake that costs contractors and facility managers real money. Two luminaires with identical 100W input and the same lumen rating can deliver completely different results on site because of one parameter: beam angle. This guide explains how beam angle controls spread and throw, shows coverage tables by mounting height, and gives you a practical method to size flood lighting for facades, parking lots, sports fields, and security perimeters.

1. What Beam Angle Actually Means

Beam angle is the angle of the light cone measured where intensity drops to 50% of its center value. A narrow beam (roughly 10 to 25 degrees) concentrates luminous flux into a tight, far-reaching cone. A wide beam (roughly 40 to 120 degrees) spreads the same lumens across a broad, short-distance area.

The key insight for buyers: at fixed lumens, a smaller beam angle produces higher center beam intensity (measured in candela). Higher candela means the light travels farther before it falls below a usable threshold. That is why a 100W narrow spotlight can illuminate a building 40 meters away while a 100W wide flood barely covers the wall ten meters in front of it.

  • Narrow beam: long throw, small bright pool, precise aiming.
  • Wide beam: short throw, large soft pool, fills open areas.
  • Beam angle is fixed by the lens or reflector, not by the LED. Mounting height and tilt change where the pool lands, not its shape.

3. Throw Distance and Coverage Area

If a fixture is aimed straight down, the pool on the ground is a circle. The coverage diameter follows a simple geometry:

  • Coverage diameter = 2 × mounting height × tan(half beam angle)
  • Radius (reach from pole base) = mounting height × tan(half beam angle)

The table below applies that formula for two common mounting heights. Treat these as vertical-aim reference values; tilting the fixture outward extends the reach along the ground.

Notice the trade-off: a Type 1 spot at 15 m covers only a 4 m circle but throws light 30 to 60 m. A Type 6 wide flood at the same height covers a 47 m circle but loses useful intensity beyond 8 m. Throw and coverage are inversely related for a given lumen package.

5. Mounting Height, Spacing, and Overlap

Beam angle alone is not enough. Fixture spacing determines whether the lit area is uniform or a patchwork of bright and dark pools. A practical rule for spacing:

  • General area lighting: space fixtures at 1.0 to 1.5 times the coverage diameter.
  • High-uniformity requirements (sports, parking aisles): space at 0.7 to 1.0 times the coverage diameter.
  • Edge aiming: aim each fixture toward the midpoint between it and the next pole to overlap pools and remove dark gaps.

Worked example. A parking lot uses Type 5 floods at 8 m height. Coverage diameter is about 15 m. For general lighting, space poles at 1.2 × 15 = 18 m apart. For uniform aisle lighting, tighten to 0.8 × 15 = 12 m. If you mistakenly specify Type 2 spots at the same height, coverage drops to 3 m and you would need roughly five times as many poles to avoid dark gaps, multiplying cost and maintenance.

6. Narrow vs Wide: When Each Wins

Neither beam type is universally better. The decision is about the shape of the target and the distance to it.

  • Choose narrow (Type 1 to 3) when: the target is far away, you must avoid spilling light onto adjacent property, you need to highlight a specific object, or light pollution control is required. Narrow beams concentrate candela and keep sky glow low.
  • Choose wide (Type 5 to 7) when: you are filling a large open area at moderate height, you want the lowest fixture count per square meter, or the work plane is close to the luminaire. Wide beams spread lumens thin, so they suit short throws.

A common error is selecting the widest beam by default to "cover more." In reality a too-wide beam overlights the ground directly below while leaving the far edge of the lot dark, because intensity falls off with distance. Match the beam to the throw you actually need.

7. Common Specification Mistakes

  • Defaulting to the widest beam. Overlights near field, starves the distance, wastes lumens.
  • Ignoring mounting height. Beam angle is fixed; changing height changes the pool size and the throw. A Type 4 at 6 m and at 12 m are different lights.
  • Forgetting uniformity. Insufficient overlap creates dark spots that read as "broken" lighting to users and cameras.
  • Mixing CCT without checking beam. Different color temperatures with the same beam can create visible seams between fixtures.
  • No tilt or aim plan. A flood aimed straight down wastes half its cone. Define aim angles in the spec.
  • Buying on watts instead of candela and beam. Watts describe power draw; candela and beam angle describe what the light actually does.

8. Procurement and Tender Checklist

When you issue a tender or request a quote, specify the beam behavior explicitly rather than hoping the supplier guesses. A complete commercial flood light specification includes:

  • Beam angle / NEMA type per zone (not just "flood").
  • Lumens and center beam candela at the target angle.
  • CCT and CRI (typically 4000K to 5000K, CRI 70 to 80 for area; 5000K to 5700K for security).
  • IP65 or IP66 rating and IK08+ impact rating for outdoor and vandal-prone sites.
  • Driver brand and surge protection (4kV to 10kV) for grid-unstable regions.
  • Dimming and controls: 0 to 10V, DALI, or photocell and motion options.
  • Adjustable tilt bracket range (at least 0 to 90 degrees) for correct aiming.
  • Photometric IES file for the exact model, validated by a lighting layout.
  • Warranty: 3 to 5 years for commercial grade.

Conclusion

Beam angle is the single most misunderstood parameter in flood light selection, and getting it wrong quietly inflates fixture counts, energy use, and complaint calls. Start from the target distance and area, pick the NEMA type that matches, set the mounting height and spacing from the coverage formula, and validate with photometric files. A narrow beam reaches far; a wide beam fills near space. Choose on geometry, not on habit.
